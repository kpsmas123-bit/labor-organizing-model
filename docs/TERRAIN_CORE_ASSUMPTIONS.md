# TERRAIN — Core Assumptions and Goals
*Working document — methodology foundation*
*Drafted June 2026*

---

## The Central Question

Given limited resources and the goal of durable political transformation in the United States, where should the labor movement invest in worker organizing — and how do we know?

---

## Assumption 1: The Dual Track

Political transformation requires two parallel tracks operating simultaneously:

**Track A — Advocacy and Mobilization (Activism)**
Electoral campaigns, issue advocacy, ballot initiatives, coalition mobilization, policy lobbying. This work is essential and well-resourced. Many organizations and individuals are already deployed here.

**Track B — Worker Organizing**
Building new union power through workplace campaigns: majority committee-building, contract fights, strikes. This work is severely under-resourced relative to Track A, and relative to what the political moment requires.

The Terrain project operates on Track B. It is not a critique of Track A — both are necessary. The argument is that Track B is neglected and that neglecting it puts a ceiling on what Track A can ever achieve.

---

## Assumption 2: Organized Labor Has Unique Leverage That Activism Alone Cannot Replicate

The labor movement possesses a form of political power unavailable to any other civic actor: **the organized withdrawal of labor**. A strike is not a protest. It is a structural interruption of economic activity that forces a direct response from employers and, at scale, from the state.

This leverage matters for political transformation in ways that compound:

1. **Crisis creation.** An organized strike in a strategic sector cannot be ignored. It forces negotiation, generates media, and demonstrates that workers have real power — not just numbers at a march.

2. **Recruitment through material wins.** Winning concrete improvements in wages, hours, and conditions is the most effective organizing tool that exists. It proves the model works. The SEIU critique of "density over member support" applies here: workers join and stay when the union delivers, not when it just exists.

3. **Whole-worker organizing.** Effective campaigns following McAlevey's model don't just organize a workplace — they map and engage the entire social world of workers: family, community, faith, neighborhood. This creates a mobilized constituency that extends beyond the workplace.

4. **Community spillover.** In sectors with genuine community-facing relationships — teachers, hospital workers, EMS — the community becomes an active campaign ally, not just a sympathetic observer. A teachers' strike is not just a labor dispute; it reorganizes parents, students, and neighbors into a political bloc.

5. **Class consciousness and solidarity.** The process of organizing — structure tests, majority committees, collective action — trains people to work together, understand power, and think politically about their lives. This is the most durable form of political education that exists.

6. **Wealth and power redistribution.** Every contract won shifts income from capital to labor. This is structural political change, not just symbolic.

**The illustrative case:** Imagine three states that, over three years, build to 90%+ supermajority strikes among teachers, hospital workers, or port workers — timed to peak in the months before a major election. The strike itself would be politically significant. But the years of lead-up would have built tens of thousands of workers into weekly organizing check-ins and political education sessions. That infrastructure doesn't dissolve after election day. That is durable power.

## Assumption 3: Strategic Resource Allocation Is Required

Not every workplace is equally valuable for this goal. Resources are finite. The question is not "does every worker deserve a union?" — they do. The question is: given limited organizing capacity and the explicit goal of political transformation, where does investment produce the greatest return?

This requires prioritization across multiple dimensions:

- **Sectoral leverage:** Can workers in this sector actually create a crisis? What kind — economic, political, or both?
- **Organizing feasibility:** How large is the unorganized workforce? How hostile is the legal environment?
- **Political terrain:** Does winning here translate into political impact given the current electoral map?
- **Infrastructure:** What already exists to build on? What kind of intervention does this place need?

Terrain is a tool for answering these questions systematically, using the best available public data.

## Where the Model Comes In

Terrain is a **quantitative terrain model** — not a decision-making algorithm.

It synthesizes public data sources (employment, union density, election results, strike history) into a structured profile of every U.S. county, designed to surface where organizing investment is most likely to produce political impact.

The model makes limited, falsifiable claims:
- Counties with large unorganized workforces in strategically leveraged sectors have more raw opportunity
- Legal environment (RTW status) materially affects organizing feasibility
- Political geography determines how much electoral impact a given organizing win translates into
- Existing union infrastructure shapes what kind of campaign a place needs

The model explicitly does **not** claim to:
- Replace on-the-ground organizer judgment
- Capture associational power, community networks, or local political dynamics that data cannot see
- Predict whether any specific campaign will succeed
- Determine which theory of change is correct

The model shows terrain. The organizer brings the goal and the judgment.

## The Novel Methodological Contribution

Most labor targeting decisions are made on the basis of:
- Existing relationships (we know people there)
- Historical presence (we've organized there before)
- Institutional inertia (that's our sector)

Terrain contributes something different: a **nationwide, county-level, theory-grounded scoring model** that makes the targeting logic explicit, transparent, and contestable.

Specific contributions:
1. **Operationalizing McAlevey's whole-worker framework quantitatively** at national scale — no prior work does this
2. **Separating strategic leverage from organizing feasibility** as distinct scored dimensions — most models collapse them
3. **Distinguishing Capital-facing and Community-facing leverage** as theoretically independent variables, not a single "strategic value" score
4. **Goal-conditional political terrain scoring** — the same county looks different depending on whether the goal is presidential swing, long-term power building, or safe-seat primary accountability
5. **Intervention type as a strategic output** — not a priority ranking but a theory-of-change classification (Type A: organize the unorganized / Type B: politically activate existing unions / Type C: partner with activated unions)

## The Limitations (Documented, Not Hidden)

**Data limitations:**
- Union density is state-level, imputed uniformly to counties — no county-level variation
- Electoral sub-scores use 2024 presidential margins as proxies for all races
- Congressional competitiveness uses presidential margin as proxy — no actual district data
- Government employment (teachers, public workers) was historically undercounted in CBP; partially corrected via QCEW government data
- LM-2 data has ~20% unresolvable ceiling (territories, PO boxes, dissolved locals)
- Strike history data exists (Cornell ILR 2019–present) but is sparse (~11% of counties have any recorded activity) and has measurement limitations for multi-site actions

**Theoretical limitations:**
- Organizability as a dimension is a draft concept — Harris critique (organic leaders may be pro-management) is an unresolved validity threat
- The model cannot capture organic networks, existing relationships, or local political dynamics
- The model cannot distinguish a high-density county with strong member engagement from one with paper membership and no mobilization capacity
- The two-actor negotiating problem: political employers (governors, school boards) respond to different incentives than private employers, and the model treats them similarly

**What this means in practice:**
The model is most useful for identifying *classes of terrain* that are systematically overlooked or overvalued. It is least useful as a precise ranking of specific counties. An organizer reading the output should treat it as a structured hypothesis to be tested against on-the-ground intelligence, not as a verdict.

## The Jobs Board Connection

The jobs board is the individual-level application of the same logic.

Where the terrain model asks "where should the movement deploy collective resources," the jobs board asks "where can one person — given their own life constraints — be most effective?"

A rank-and-file worker who takes a job in a high-SVS sector in a high-OOS county and builds a majority committee is doing the same work as a strategic campaign investment — just from the bottom up. A staff organizer who takes a role in a battleground MSA is deploying their individual capacity where it has the highest multiplier.

The jobs board makes the same assumptions — whole-worker organizing, strategic sector prioritization, political terrain — but filtered through personal reality: location, experience level, willingness to relocate, career stage.

---

## Summary Statement

The Terrain model is grounded in three claims:

1. Political transformation in the United States requires rebuilding the organized labor movement, not just mobilizing existing activists
2. Rebuilding the labor movement requires strategic investment in actual workplace organizing, not just union density maintenance
3. Strategic investment requires a principled targeting framework that can identify where organizing produces the greatest leverage — economic, political, and organizational — given finite resources

The model is the targeting framework. It is open-source, methodologically transparent, grounded in peer-reviewed labor theory, and designed to be contested, improved, and used.
