Methodology

How the model works, what it measures, and where it falls short.

Section 1

The Stakes

The American labor movement was built through profound sacrifice, skill, and strategy. In the early decades of the twentieth century, workers organized against horrific exploitation and working conditions — despite illegal suppression, physical violence, and institutional hostility. They won because they organized themselves, built relationships, solidarity, and organization.

That organizing success translated into immense political power and established organized labor as a central force in American politics and the Democratic coalition for a generation.

That foundation has been under sustained attack since the 1970s. The deliberate dismantling of labor power that accelerated under Reagan's neoliberal program pushed the movement away from the fundamentals that built it. In the decades that followed, organized labor relied increasingly on existing labor law, historical party relationships, and political contributions rather than returning to the on-the-ground workplace organizing that first brought unions to power.

We are once again in a political predicament that demands a return to the fundamentals of organizing. Real, long-term worker organizing builds solidarity and transforms not just workplaces but communities across the country.

In the face of rising political hostility, the movement must be strategic about where it invests. Not every sector in every county is equally capable of creating the kind of crisis that forces real political change.

Our theory of change rests on two claims. First, that durable power is built through well-placed, interpersonal, organized solidarity — irrespective of institutional permission. Second, that winning material gains for workers in the workplace is the fastest and most effective way to build a movement capable of addressing our broader social and political needs. When workers organize, they win. When workers win, we all win.

Section 2

Three Core Assumptions

The model is built on three assumptions. Each is contestable. All are documented.

Assumption 1

Organized workers have structural power

Organized workers possess a form of political power that is structurally distinct from any other. The right to withhold labor cannot be fully legislated away. When workers are organized enough, they can act collectively even under hostile legal conditions — and win. That power is real, not dependent on institutional permission, and cannot be neutralized by money or messaging alone.

But that power does not activate on its own. It has to be built.

Assumption 2

Building that power requires deep organizing

Jane McAlevey identifies three distinct forms of political activity: advocacy, mobilization, and organizing.[1] Advocacy directs resources at policymakers. Mobilization activates an existing base of supporters. Organizing is different — it is the long-term, relational work of bringing unengaged workers into a campaign, training them, building worker-led structures, and winning material gains that prove collective action works. Those wins expand the base, develop new leadership, and create independent power that outlasts any single campaign or election cycle. The labor movement has relied heavily on advocacy and mobilization while under-investing in deep organizing. That imbalance is limiting what the movement can achieve.

If organizing is the path to durable power, the next question is: where does organizing produce the greatest leverage?

Assumption 3

Not all organizing produces equal political leverage

Every worker in every county deserves a union; that right is not conditional on predicted strategic value. However, to build a labor movement large enough to win in the face of rising political hostility, we have to be strategic and prioritize. Workers in key sectors, demographics, and locations have an outsized political impact created by their organizing.[5] We need to focus on these highest-impact campaigns first so we can build a winning coalition that can deliver for all workers and families.

That is where this model comes in.

Section 3

What the Model Does

What the Model Measures

Terrain looks at two things for every U.S. county. First, the relative leverage of workers in that county — analyzing 42 sectors scored on their capacity to create economic and community crisis, then adjusting those scores for the actual composition of each county's workforce. Second, the electoral status of that county — how it fits into the broader political map, whether it sits in a competitive state or district, and how much organizing there would translate into political impact.

What the Model Does Not Claim

The model does not replace organizer judgment. It cannot see associational networks, existing relationships, community trust, or the conditions that determine whether a campaign actually wins. It does not predict whether any specific campaign will succeed, determine which candidates are worth supporting, or resolve questions that require local knowledge and human strategic judgment.

A Note on AI

Much of the software coding and technical design of this project was built with Anthropic AI tools. All research decisions, methodological design, and theoretical framing were made by human researchers. We believe in an open and honest conversation about the role, limits, and possibilities of AI tools in academic research — and we think that conversation is better served by transparency than by silence.

The Jobs Board

The map is designed for people thinking about where to deploy organizational resources. The jobs board applies the same logic at the individual level: given your skills, your location, and your life, where can you have the most impact as an organizer or rank-and-file worker? Same framework, different scale of application.

Section 4

Score 1: Strategic Leverage Score

What Strategic Leverage Measures

Not all workers have equal structural power. A hospital worker has different leverage than a retail worker. A port worker has different leverage than a software engineer. The Strategic Leverage Score attempts to measure that difference systematically — first at the sector level, then adjusted for the actual composition of each county's workforce.

The Sector Strategic Value Score

Each of the 42 sectors in the model is scored on four variables. Capital Crisis-Creating Reach measures how much a work stoppage in this sector would disrupt capital flows, supply chains, or critical economic infrastructure — scored from 0 to 25 points across four levels: none, local, state, and national. Community Crisis-Creating Reach measures how much a work stoppage would disrupt the daily life of communities — essential services, healthcare, transit, education — scored on the same scale. Community-Facing Reach measures how deeply a sector is embedded in community trust networks, not just whether workers can cause disruption but whether they can build lasting solidarity with the people they serve, scored 0 to 15 points. Non-Offshoreable measures whether jobs in this sector can be relocated, scored 0 to 5 points.

Two interaction bonuses capture cases where variables reinforce each other. A sector scoring above zero on both capital and community crisis reach receives a Dual-Crisis bonus of 5 points. A sector scoring above zero on both community crisis reach and community-facing reach receives a Whole-Worker bonus of 5 points.

SVS = Capital Reach + Community Reach + Community-Facing Reach + Non-Offshoreable + Dual-Crisis Bonus + Whole-Worker Bonus Maximum: 80 points

Methodology Note — Sector Scoring

Sector scoring was conducted by human researchers based on best judgment, case study review, and existing labor literature. All sector codings are documented in the project repository and are subject to review and challenge. Future research will include cross-coding by multiple independent experts to reduce individual bias and strengthen inter-rater reliability.

Two additional limitations: sector scores are static and do not automatically update as industries change; and the model does not yet distinguish between latent structural leverage and current active organizing capacity. Sectors where this gap is most significant are flagged in the sector table with an asterisk.

SLS-Capital: Absolute Crisis Potential

The first county-level sub-score multiplies each sector's Capital Crisis-Creating Reach score by the raw number of workers employed in that sector in that county, then sums across all sectors. The result is normalized to a 0 to 100 scale using an absolute benchmark — the theoretical maximum weighted employment score across all sectors nationally — so that a score of 75 in Pennsylvania means the same as a 75 in Texas.

Raw employment count drives this score deliberately. The capacity to disrupt capital flows, supply chains, or critical economic infrastructure scales with the absolute number of workers involved. Ten thousand port workers have more capital-facing leverage than one thousand port workers regardless of what share of the local economy they represent.

SLS-Capital = Σ(cap_reach_score × raw_employment_in_sector) / national_benchmark Normalized to 0–100

SLS-Community: Relative Crisis Potential

The second county-level sub-score multiplies each sector's Community Crisis-Creating Reach score by that sector's share of the county's total workforce, then sums across all sectors. Share of workforce drives this score rather than raw count.

A hospital that employs 30 percent of a rural county's workforce creates qualitatively different community mobilization potential than a hospital employing 4 percent of a large metro county's workforce, even if the metro hospital employs more people in absolute terms. When a sector is structurally central to a community's daily life, a work stoppage is impossible to ignore. That structural centrality is what this score captures.

SLS-Community = Σ(comm_reach_score × employment_share_in_sector) Normalized to 0–100

Why Two Scores Rather Than One

Collapsing these into a single number would obscure a distinction that matters strategically. A county can score high on SLS-Capital and low on SLS-Community — a logistics hub in a large metro area, for example — or high on SLS-Community and low on SLS-Capital, such as a rural county where the hospital is the dominant employer. Those are different types of terrain requiring different organizing approaches. The model keeps them separate so users can see both dimensions independently.

Section 5

Score 2: Political Terrain Score

What Political Terrain Measures

Not all geographies are equally consequential for the political goals of the labor movement. The American electoral system distributes political weight unevenly — through the Electoral College, Senate malapportionment, and legislative district boundaries that do not reflect population equally. The Political Terrain Score attempts to capture that unequal distribution honestly, across two independent dimensions.

P1: Electoral Leverage

The first sub-score measures how much this specific county matters for outcomes that determine labor law and policy. It is calculated as the state's tipping point weight multiplied by the inverse of the county's electoral margin. State tipping point weight reflects the probability that a given state provides the decisive votes in a presidential, Senate, or state legislative race. County margin reflects how contested the county itself is. A county in a decisive state with a narrow margin scores high. A heavily partisan county in a safe state scores low regardless of its size.

The model calculates three variants of this score independently: presidential, congressional, and state legislative. Congressional and state legislative scores are aggregated to the county level using Census district-to-county relationship files, averaging across districts where boundaries do not align with county lines.[2]

P1 = state_tipping_point_weight × (1 / county_electoral_margin)

This is a simplified proxy for a full two-stage empirical Banzhaf power index (Banzhaf, 1968).[3] That upgrade is planned for a future version of the model.

Organizing in counties that swing elections produces electoral returns that extend beyond union households into surrounding communities — research suggests newly organized workers generate measurable progressive vote gains in their commuting zones, with the majority of the effect operating through indirect community channels.[4]

P2: Incumbent Alignment

The second sub-score measures whether the existing political environment is aligned with labor's agenda — based on demonstrated behavior rather than party label or endorsement history.

Two signals drive this score. The first is a key vote alignment score: for each incumbent representing a county, we calculate the share of a defined set of labor-relevant votes cast in a pro-labor direction. The second signal is the inverse of the business funding share for each incumbent — the ratio of business contributions to total campaign contributions. High business funding concentration is a reliable negative signal for labor alignment regardless of party.[6]

P2 = (key_vote_score × 0.60) + (inverse_business_funding_share × 0.40)

Data Sources Considered and Rejected

AFL-CIO legislative scorecards were excluded because they encode the institutional AFL-CIO's strategic priorities, which do not always reflect the organizing-centered theory of change this model is built on.[7]

Labor PAC contributions were excluded as a positive signal because they reflect existing labor leadership targeting decisions — precisely the status quo this model is designed to complement and occasionally challenge.

Section 6

Reading the Map

Four Types of Terrain

The two scores produce a simple but useful strategic picture. A county's Strategic Leverage Score tells you how much structural power organized workers there would have. The Political Terrain Score tells you how much organizing there would translate into political impact.

↑ Strategic Leverage (high → low)

High Leverage / High Alignment

Deploy now — organized workers here have both structural power and a political environment capable of translating that power into change.

High Leverage / Low Alignment

Primary target or challenger opportunity — high leverage terrain held by incumbents with demonstrated anti-labor records. The model's most novel output.

Low Leverage / High Alignment

Long-term power building — favorable political conditions, weaker structural terrain. Rewards sustained investment over time.

Low Leverage / Low Alignment

Not a current priority given scarce resources.

← High Alignment

Low Alignment →

Shading within quadrants

Within each quadrant, counties are shaded dark to light by national significance — how far above the threshold each score sits, weighted by state tipping point. A dark red county is a top national target. A pale red county is the same terrain type but lower national stakes.

Quadrant thresholds are set at the midpoint of each score's 0 to 100 range by default and are documented in the project's open configuration files. They will be revisited through regression validation against known organizing campaigns.

What This Does Not Resolve

The scores are independent of each other by design. The model does not combine them into a single ranking because doing so would require imposing a weighting between structural leverage and political terrain — a decision that depends on the user's theory of change and strategic goals, not on the data. The model surfaces both dimensions and leaves that judgment where it belongs.

Section 7

Known Limitations

These are not disclaimers. They are specifications of where the data ends and organizer judgment must begin.

The Census Bureau's County Business Patterns dataset excludes government employees by design. Teachers, public hospital workers, transit operators, sanitation workers, and public administrators do not appear in CBP counts. This means counties with large public sector workforces — state capitals, county seats, districts with major public university systems — show lower Strategic Leverage Scores than their actual structural importance warrants. BLS QCEW data provides partial correction for public sector sectors in the model, but the gap is not fully resolved. Future versions will expand QCEW government data integration to reduce this gap.

The 42 sector scores were assigned by human researchers based on best judgment, case study review, and existing labor literature. NAICS industry classifications do not always map cleanly onto how workers experience their industries. Sector scores are static and do not automatically update as industries change.

Most significantly, the model does not yet distinguish between latent structural leverage and current active organizing capacity. A utility worker has enormous latent leverage but current organizing density in that sector may be low. An auto worker may have strong historical organizing infrastructure but face structural vulnerabilities that limit future leverage. The model scores structural position but cannot yet see the gap between what a sector could do and what it is currently capable of doing. Future research will include cross-coding by multiple independent experts to strengthen inter-rater reliability.

All three electoral leverage calculations are grounded in county-level 2024 presidential election margins. Congressional and state legislative scores apply those margins as proxies for district-level competitiveness. Presidential margins do not perfectly predict congressional or state legislative race outcomes, particularly in gerrymandered districts. All three electoral sub-scores should be treated as directional signals rather than precise measurements. Future versions will incorporate actual district-level race results and Cook Political Report competitiveness ratings.

P2 Incumbent Alignment depends on data sources with uneven coverage across states. VoteSmart and ProPublica cover federal legislators comprehensively but state legislative coverage varies. FollowTheMoney covers state campaign finance broadly but reporting requirements differ by state. Counties in states with weaker coverage will have less reliable P2 scores. The model flags coverage level for each county — full, partial, or unknown.

The model has no signal for existing community infrastructure — labor councils, interfaith coalitions, tenant organizations, mutual aid networks, or other forms of associational power. It cannot measure political relationships, trust networks, or the organizational readiness of a community to support a campaign. No public dataset captures these reliably at national scale. This is the gap most likely to be filled by organizer judgment rather than future data work.

The electoral leverage calculations rely in part on research that measures labor's political impact through Democratic vote share — a proxy that has historically been strong but is becoming more complex. The model attempts to address this through the P2 Incumbent Alignment score, which measures demonstrated legislative behavior rather than party label. However, the underlying electoral leverage calculation still depends on competitive margin data that reflects the two-party system. Future versions will seek to measure political alignment in ways less dependent on the two-party frame.

Section 10

Further Research and Future Updates

  1. 1
    Regression validation against known campaigns

    The most important near-term research priority is testing the model's predictive validity against a sample of known organizing campaigns from the last decade. We plan to assess whether SLS and PTS scores predict which campaigns produced the greatest political impact, and use the results to update scoring weights and thresholds.

  2. 2
    Latent versus active capacity distinction

    A future version will introduce a scored dimension distinguishing between sectors with high latent structural potential and those with active current organizing capacity, drawing on NLRB petition activity, recent strike history, and current union density.

  3. 3
    Intervention type classification

    A third layer of the model — currently in design — will classify counties by what kind of organizing intervention the terrain calls for: organize the unorganized, politically activate existing unions, or partner with already-activated unions.

  4. 4
    Full two-stage electoral leverage calculation

    The current P1 score uses a simplified proxy. A future version will implement a full two-stage empirical Banzhaf power index incorporating actual electoral simulation data.

  5. 5
    Expanded political alignment coverage

    Key vote tracking and campaign finance data coverage is currently uneven across states. Expanding and standardizing that coverage is an ongoing data pipeline priority.

  6. 6
    Cross-coding of sector scores

    Sector codings will be reviewed by multiple independent experts to strengthen inter-rater reliability and reduce individual bias.

  7. 7
    Non-partisan political alignment measurement

    Future versions will develop alignment measures less dependent on the two-party frame as the relationship between union membership and two-party voting continues to evolve.
