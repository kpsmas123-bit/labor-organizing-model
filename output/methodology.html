Section 1

The Stakes

The American labor movement was built through profound sacrifice, skill, and strategy. In the early decades of the twentieth century, workers organized against horrific exploitation and working conditions — despite illegal suppression, physical violence, and institutional hostility. They won because they organized themselves, built relationships, solidarity, and organization.

That organizing success translated into immense political power and established organized labor as a central force in American politics and the Democratic coalition for a generation.

That foundation has been under sustained attack since the 1970s. The deliberate dismantling of labor power that accelerated under Reagan's neoliberal program pushed the movement away from the fundamentals that built it. In the decades that followed, organized labor relied increasingly on existing labor law, historical party relationships, and political contributions rather than returning to the on-the-ground workplace organizing that first brought unions to power.

We are once again in a political predicament that demands a return to the fundamentals of organizing. Real, long-term worker organizing builds solidarity and transforms not just workplaces but communities across the country.

In the face of rising political hostility, the movement must be strategic about where it invests. Not every sector in every county is equally capable of creating the kind of crisis that forces real political change.

Our theory of change rests on two claims. First, that durable power is built through well-placed, interpersonal, organized solidarity — irrespective of institutional permission. Second, that winning material gains for workers in the workplace is the fastest and most effective way to build a movement capable of addressing our broader social and political needs. When workers organize, they win. When workers win, we all win.

Section 2

Three Core Assumptions

The model is built on three assumptions. Each is contestable. All are documented.

Assumption 1

Organized workers have structural power

Organized workers possess a form of political power that is structurally distinct from any other. The right to withhold labor cannot be fully legislated away. When workers are organized enough, they can act collectively even under hostile legal conditions — and win. That power is real, not dependent on institutional permission, and cannot be neutralized by money or messaging alone.

But that power does not activate on its own. It has to be built.

Assumption 2

Building that power requires deep organizing

Jane McAlevey identifies three distinct forms of political activity: advocacy, mobilization, and organizing.[1] Advocacy directs resources at policymakers. Mobilization activates an existing base of supporters. Organizing is different — it is the long-term, relational work of bringing unengaged workers into a campaign, training them, building worker-led structures, and winning material gains that prove collective action works. Those wins expand the base, develop new leadership, and create independent power that outlasts any single campaign or election cycle. The labor movement has relied heavily on advocacy and mobilization while under-investing in deep organizing. That imbalance is limiting what the movement can achieve.

If organizing is the path to durable power, the next question is: where does organizing produce the greatest leverage?

Assumption 3

Not all organizing produces equal political leverage

Every worker in every county deserves a union; that right is not conditional on predicted strategic value. However, to build a labor movement large enough to win in the face of rising political hostility, we have to be strategic and prioritize. Workers in key sectors, demographics, and locations have an outsized political impact created by their organizing.[5] We need to focus on these highest-impact campaigns first so we can build a winning coalition that can deliver for all workers and families.

That is where this model comes in.

Section 3

What the Model Does

What the Model Measures

Terrain scores every U.S. county on two independent dimensions:

Strategic Leverage Score (SLS) — How much structural power would organized workers here have?

Computed separately for two types of leverage:

  • Capital Leverage (SLS-Capital): The absolute crisis-creating potential against capital flows — ports, logistics, energy infrastructure, concentrated manufacturing. Measured as the sum of each sector's capital crisis reach score weighted by raw employment count, normalized against the national benchmark county.
  • Community Leverage (SLS-Community): The relational crisis-creating potential within the community — hospitals, schools, public transit, home health. Measured as the weighted share of the county workforce in community-facing sectors, so that a hospital employing 40% of a rural county scores differently than the same hospital at 2% of a metro workforce.

Political Terrain Score (PTS) — How much does organizing here translate into political impact?

Two sub-scores, each computed under two lenses:

  • P1 Electoral Leverage: How decisive is this county's geography in consequential elections? Formula: state_tipping_weight × (1 / abs(county_margin)). Counties in decisive states with tight margins score highest.
  • P2 Incumbent Alignment: How aligned are this county's current elected officials with labor's legislative agenda? Computed from voting records on defined key votes and ideology scores derived from legislative behavior patterns.

Two Lenses

The model produces scores under two strategic frames:

  • National Lens: Uses federal electoral tipping point weights and federal incumbent alignment (Congress). For organizations deploying national resources.
  • State Lens: Uses state legislative chamber control weights and state incumbent alignment. For state federations and regional organizations.

A Note on AI

SLS scores are computed by a deterministic formula applied to BLS employment data and the sector scoring rubric. No AI is involved in the primary model. AI (Claude Sonnet) is used to generate MSA narrative summaries shown in the detail panel — those summaries are labeled as AI-generated and should be treated accordingly.

The Jobs Board

The companion jobs board at laborterrain.org/jobs matches individuals to labor movement roles using the same terrain data. The model shows terrain. The user brings the goal.

Section 4

Score 1: Strategic Leverage Score

What Strategic Leverage Measures

Strategic leverage is the structural capacity of workers to disrupt the conditions that matter to their employer or to the broader political economy. Two types of disruption are measured independently because they operate through different mechanisms and require different organizing approaches.

The Sector Strategic Value Score

Every sector is scored on four components derived from the organizing literature. Capital Crisis Reach measures the capacity to disrupt capital flows through work stoppage — scored 0, 10, 15, or 25 points. Community Crisis Reach measures the capacity to create community-facing crisis — scored on the same scale. Community Facing Reach measures pre-existing relationships of trust with the public — scored 0, 5, 10, or 15 points. Non-Offshoreable measures whether work is structurally tied to physical U.S. location — scored 0, 3, or 5 points. Maximum sector score: 80 points.

Two interaction bonuses capture cases where variables reinforce each other. A sector scoring above zero on both capital and community crisis reach receives a Dual-Crisis bonus of 5 points (+5 if Capital Reach > 0 AND Community Reach > 0). A sector scoring above zero on both community crisis reach and community-facing reach receives a Whole-Worker bonus of 5 points (+5 if Community Reach > 0 AND Community Facing > 0).

SVS = Capital Reach + Community Reach + Community-Facing Reach + Non-Offshoreable + Dual-Crisis Bonus + Whole-Worker Bonus Maximum: 80 points

Methodology Note — Sector Scoring

Sector scoring was conducted by human researchers based on best judgment, case study review, and existing labor literature. All sector codings are documented in the project repository and are subject to review and challenge. Future research will include cross-coding by multiple independent experts to reduce individual bias and strengthen inter-rater reliability.

Two additional limitations: sector scores are static and do not automatically update as industries change; and the model does not yet distinguish between latent structural leverage and current active organizing capacity. Sectors where this gap is most significant are flagged in the sector table with an asterisk.

SLS-Capital: Absolute Crisis Potential

The first county-level sub-score multiplies each sector's Capital Crisis Reach score by the raw number of workers employed in that sector in that county, then sums across all sectors. Scale matters here — a port employing 50,000 workers can halt more supply chain activity than a port employing 5,000.

SLS-Capital = Σ(cap_reach_score[sector] × employment[sector]) / 210,000Calibrated to LA County's raw sum (17,673,345) → score 84.2

Normalization benchmark of 210,000 places Los Angeles County (the national maximum) at 84.2 on a 0–100 scale. Counties with major port, logistics, or energy workforces score highest. Grounded in: Womack (2005) on technically strategic positions; Silver (2003) on workplace bargaining power and spatial fixes.

SLS-Community: Relative Crisis Potential

The second county-level sub-score multiplies each sector's Community Crisis Reach score by that sector's share of the county's total workforce, then sums across all sectors. Share matters here — a hospital employing 40% of a rural county's workforce is structurally central to that community in a way the same hospital cannot be at 2% of a large metro workforce.

SLS-Community = Σ(comm_reach_score[sector] × (employment[sector] / total_employment)) × 4

The ×4 normalization scales to 0–100 where a county with 100% of workers in a national-level community crisis sector scores 100. Counties with concentrated healthcare, education, or public transit workforces score highest. Grounded in: McAlevey (2016) on whole-worker organizing and community mobilization; Fox-Hodess (2023) on social contingency in organizing.

Why Two Scores Rather Than One

Collapsing these into a single number would obscure a distinction that matters strategically. A county can score high on SLS-Capital and low on SLS-Community — a logistics hub in a large metro area, for example — or high on SLS-Community and low on SLS-Capital, such as a rural county where the hospital is the dominant employer. Those are different types of terrain requiring different organizing approaches. The model keeps them separate so users can see both dimensions independently.

Section 5

Score 2: Political Terrain Score

What Political Terrain Measures

Political terrain answers: how much does labor organizing in this county change the political environment? Two components are measured independently.

P1: Electoral Leverage

P1 measures how decisive this county's geography is in elections that determine labor's legislative environment.

P1 = state_tipping_weight × (1 / abs(county_margin))PA tipping weight: 0.28
Centre County margin: +2.83
→ P1 = 0.28 × (1/2.83) = 35.3

State tipping weight is the probability that this state is the decisive state in the relevant election, derived from historical swing state analysis and current electoral projections. Pennsylvania = 0.28; Georgia = 0.09; California = 0.005. County margin is the absolute margin (D–R) in the most recent relevant election. Tight margins produce high P1 scores even in states with lower tipping weights.

Under the National Lens, P1 uses presidential electoral weights. Under the State Lens, P1 uses state legislative chamber tipping weights — how close is chamber control in this county's state?

The P1 distribution is extremely right-skewed. 75% of all U.S. counties have P1 < 0.28. The top 3% of counties by P1 are the genuinely decisive geographies. Centre County, Pennsylvania — home of Penn State and one of the tightest-margined counties in the most decisive swing state — scores P1 = 35.3.

This is a simplified proxy for a full two-stage empirical Banzhaf power index (Banzhaf, 1968).[3] That upgrade is planned for a future version of the model.

Organizing in counties that swing elections produces electoral returns that extend beyond union households into surrounding communities — research suggests newly organized workers generate measurable progressive vote gains in their commuting zones, with the majority of the effect operating through indirect community channels.[4]

P2: Incumbent Alignment

P2 measures how aligned this county's current elected officials are with labor's legislative agenda. Low P2 + high SLS + high P1 identifies the highest-value organizing targets: places where the workforce has leverage, the geography is decisive, and the incumbent is hostile to labor.

P2 = key_vote_score × 0.60 + inverse_ideology_score × 0.40

Key vote component (60%): Each legislator is scored on 4 federal key votesPRO Act, Sanders MW, Abruzzo confirmation, National Apprenticeship Act — all 117th Congress directly relevant to labor organizing rights. All roll call numbers verified against House Clerk XML and Senate.gov XML records.

Vote Chamber / Roll Call Date
PRO Act House Roll Call 70 March 9, 2021
Sanders $15 Minimum Wage Amendment Senate Roll Call 74 March 5, 2021
Abruzzo NLRB General Counsel Confirmation Senate Roll Call 273 July 21, 2021
National Apprenticeship Act House Roll Call 31 February 5, 2021

Ideology component (40%): Legislative behavior ideology scores derived from bill sponsorship and cosponsorship patterns (GovTrack, 119th Congress). Normalized so that progressive = high signal, conservative = low signal. County P2 is aggregated from individual legislator scores weighted by district-county overlap from the Census district-county relationship file.

Current P2 coverage: Federal P2: 533 legislators scored, 3,142 counties with alignment data. State P2: in progress — using DIME CFscores (Stanford) for current state legislators where available.

Data Sources Considered and Rejected

AFL-CIO legislative scorecards were excluded because they encode the institutional AFL-CIO's strategic priorities, which do not always reflect the organizing-centered theory of change this model is built on.[7]

Labor PAC contributions were excluded as a positive signal because they reflect existing labor leadership targeting decisions — precisely the status quo this model is designed to complement and occasionally challenge.

Section 6

Reading the Map

Six Types of Strategic Terrain

The model classifies every county into one of six categories based on the combination of SLS, P1, and P2 scores. The classification answers two questions simultaneously: how much organizing leverage exists here, and what strategic posture does the political terrain require?

Tier 1 — Transform

High SLS + High P1 + Low P2 (hostile incumbent)

The highest-priority organizing terrain. Workers here have structural leverage AND the geography is electorally decisive AND the incumbent is misaligned with labor. Organizing campaigns here can simultaneously build worker power and shift the political environment by targeting a hostile incumbent who cannot afford to lose organized worker opposition. These are primary challenge and electoral accountability targets as much as organizing targets.

  • Tier 1 — Capital: High capital leverage (ports, logistics, energy)
  • Tier 1 — Community: High community leverage (hospitals, schools, transit)

Tier 2 — Activate

High SLS + High P1 + High P2 (aligned incumbent)

High leverage, decisive terrain, but the incumbent is already aligned with labor. The strategic posture shifts from challenge to mobilization — protecting incumbents, turning out existing members, defending labor-aligned policy. Organizing here builds organizational capacity in territory where the political return is defending gains rather than making them.

  • Tier 2 — Capital: High capital leverage, aligned incumbent
  • Tier 2 — Community: High community leverage, aligned incumbent

Tier 3 — Electoral Terrain

Low SLS + High P1 (decisive geography, organizing base needed)

The geography is electorally decisive but the organizing infrastructure does not yet exist at scale to move the county. These are long-term investment targets — building the worker organization that currently isn't there. Centre County, Pennsylvania and Green County, Wisconsin are canonical examples: smaller workforces, extreme electoral leverage, organizing bases that do not yet match their political importance.

Tier 4 — Lower Priority

Neither SLS nor P1 threshold met at current thresholds.

Not a current strategic priority given current organizing capacity constraints. The model does not claim these workers are unimportant — every worker deserves representation. It claims that with finite resources, the above categories offer higher expected political return per organizing dollar.

The Critical Finding: No County is Tier 1 in Both Dimensions

When we ran the model on all 3,143 U.S. counties, no county simultaneously cleared all three thresholds (SLS-Capital ≥ 2.5, SLS-Community ≥ 35, P1 ≥ 5). The largest labor markets are concentrated in states that do not decide elections. The counties that do decide elections are smaller, with workforces dominated by community-facing sectors.

This is not a modeling failure. It is an empirical description of American political and economic geography. It confirms McAlevey's sectoral argument independently: if you want to organize where it matters electorally, organize hospitals and schools in Pennsylvania, Michigan, and Wisconsin — not ports and logistics hubs in California.

Two Strategic Lenses

Use the National lens to identify where national labor organizations should deploy resources to move federal elections and federal policy. P1 uses presidential electoral tipping weights.

Use the State lens to identify where state federations and regional organizations should invest. P1 uses state legislative chamber tipping weights — how close is chamber control in this state's legislature?

The scores are independent of each other by design. The model does not combine them into a single ranking because doing so would require imposing a weighting between structural leverage and political terrain — a decision that depends on the user's theory of change and strategic goals, not on the data. The model surfaces both dimensions and leaves that judgment where it belongs.

Section 7

Known Limitations

These are not disclaimers. They are specifications of where the data ends and organizer judgment must begin.

SLS scores use Census Bureau County Business Patterns data, which excludes government employees. Counties where government employment is dominant — state capitals, counties with large military installations, Washington D.C. — will have understated SLS scores. QCEW government data has been ingested separately for public sector employment but the full integration is in progress.

The 42 sector scores were assigned by human researchers based on best judgment, case study review, and existing labor literature. NAICS industry classifications do not always map cleanly onto how workers experience their industries. Sector scores are static and do not automatically update as industries change.

Most significantly, the model does not yet distinguish between latent structural leverage and current active organizing capacity. A utility worker has enormous latent leverage but current organizing density in that sector may be low. An auto worker may have strong historical organizing infrastructure but face structural vulnerabilities that limit future leverage. The model scores structural position but cannot yet see the gap between what a sector could do and what it is currently capable of doing. Future research will include cross-coding by multiple independent experts to strengthen inter-rater reliability.

The current P1 formula uses presidential electoral margins as the primary proxy for congressional and state legislative district competitiveness. True district-level margins require separate ingestion of district-level election results, which is in progress. Counties that span multiple competitive districts may be under- or over-scored.

P2 Incumbent Alignment depends on data sources with uneven coverage across states. VoteSmart and ProPublica cover federal legislators comprehensively but state legislative coverage varies. FollowTheMoney covers state campaign finance broadly but reporting requirements differ by state. Counties in states with weaker coverage will have less reliable P2 scores. The model flags coverage level for each county — full, partial, or unknown.

The model has no signal for existing community infrastructure — labor councils, interfaith coalitions, tenant organizations, mutual aid networks, or other forms of associational power. It cannot measure political relationships, trust networks, or the organizational readiness of a community to support a campaign. No public dataset captures these reliably at national scale. This is the gap most likely to be filled by organizer judgment rather than future data work.

The electoral leverage calculations rely in part on research that measures labor's political impact through Democratic vote share — a proxy that has historically been strong but is becoming more complex. The model attempts to address this through the P2 Incumbent Alignment score, which measures demonstrated legislative behavior rather than party label. However, the underlying electoral leverage calculation still depends on competitive margin data that reflects the two-party system. Future versions will seek to measure political alignment in ways less dependent on the two-party frame.

Federal P2 scores are complete for all 533 current federal legislators across 3,142 counties. State P2 scores use DIME CFscores (Stanford, 1979–2024) matched to current state legislators via Open States. State legislative ideology data is most reliable for the 2010–2020 period; 2022–2024 state legislative coverage is sparse. State P2 is flagged as coverage_type = cfscore_only in the underlying data.

Section 10

Further Research and Future Updates

  1. 1
    Regression validation against known campaigns

    The most important near-term research priority is testing the model's predictive validity against a sample of known organizing campaigns from the last decade. We plan to assess whether SLS and PTS scores predict which campaigns produced the greatest political impact, and use the results to update scoring weights and thresholds.

  2. 2
    Latent versus active capacity distinction

    A future version will introduce a scored dimension distinguishing between sectors with high latent structural potential and those with active current organizing capacity, drawing on NLRB petition activity, recent strike history, and current union density.

  3. 3
    Intervention type classification

    A third layer of the model — currently in design — will classify counties by what kind of organizing intervention the terrain calls for: organize the unorganized, politically activate existing unions, or partner with already-activated unions.

  4. 4
    Full two-stage electoral leverage calculation

    The current P1 score uses a simplified proxy. A future version will implement a full two-stage empirical Banzhaf power index incorporating actual electoral simulation data.

  5. 5
    Expanded political alignment coverage

    Key vote tracking and campaign finance data coverage is currently uneven across states. Expanding and standardizing that coverage is an ongoing data pipeline priority.

  6. 6
    Cross-coding of sector scores

    Sector codings will be reviewed by multiple independent experts to strengthen inter-rater reliability and reduce individual bias.

  7. 7
    Non-partisan political alignment measurement

    Future versions will develop alignment measures less dependent on the two-party frame as the relationship between union membership and two-party voting continues to evolve.

About This Project

Terrain was created by Sam Kaplan Pettus, an independent researcher and political organizer. Sam holds a degree in Political Science from UC Berkeley and built Terrain to surface where labor and political power-building intersect across every U.S. county.
