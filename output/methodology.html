What this model does

Ranks U.S. counties by strategic terrain for labor organizing, filtered by electoral goal and intervention type. Uses the best available structural data as a prioritization tool — not a replacement for on-the-ground organizer judgment.

What this model doesn't do

It does not measure associational power, organic leader networks, employer-specific vulnerability, or local political dynamics. Those are the domain of organizer judgment. Omitted variables are documented in Section 4.

"The model does not pick the strategic goal. The user brings the goal. The model shows the terrain."

Section 2

Scoring Components

Every score in the model is documented below. Click any cell for the full specification — formula, data sources, known limitations, and theoretical grounding.

Scoring Component 1
Organizing Opportunity Score
Formula
OOS = (Sectoral Value Score × 0.55) + (Organizing Potential Score × 0.45)
Plain Language

The Organizing Opportunity Score is the model's primary output for a county's organizing terrain. A score of 100 means maximum strategic value — a large, strategically concentrated workforce that is substantially unorganized. A score near zero means little structural leverage and little unorganized workforce to build from.

Range: 0–100. Scores are scaled using an absolute benchmark, not relative to other counties, so they are stable across filtered views.

What is intentionally excluded

Electoral scores and the Infrastructure Score are not inputs to the Organizing Opportunity Score. This is a deliberate design choice: the organizing question (where can we build worker power?) must be answered independently of the electoral question (where does that power matter most?). Combining them would obscure both answers.

The Composite Ranking combines the OOS with electoral sub-scores only after the user specifies a goal. Infrastructure Score is kept as a reference dimension that drives Intervention Type classification but does not inflate the organizing score.

Scoring Component 2
Sectoral Value Score (County-Level)
Formula
County SVS = Σ (sector_SVS × county_employment_in_sector)
→ scaled to 0–100 using absolute benchmark
Plain Language

A county scores high on this dimension only if it has both a strategically valuable sector and a significant workforce in it. The two factors multiply. A county full of retail workers scores low even if it sits in a swing state. A county with a single large hospital serving a rural region scores high.

Scaling approach: An absolute benchmark (the theoretical maximum weighted employment score across all sectors) anchors the scale. This means scores are comparable across counties and across filtered views — a 75 in Pennsylvania means the same as a 75 in Texas.

Known Limitation
⚠️ Public sector employment is understated in this score. The County Business Patterns dataset (CBP), from which employment figures derive, excludes government employees by design. Counties with large government workforces — DC, state capitals, county seats — will show lower Sectoral Value Scores than their actual structural importance warrants. This is a confirmed data gap, not a model error. BLS QCEW data partially compensates but does not fully resolve it.

Scoring Component 4
Organizing Potential Score
Calculation
Unorganized workforce = Total employment − Estimated union members
→ scaled by county employment share of national total
→ normalized to 0–100
Plain Language

The Organizing Potential Score answers the question: how much untapped workforce is here to organize? It is a measure of quantity of opportunity, not quality. Quality is captured by the Sectoral Value Score. The two dimensions together form the Organizing Opportunity Score.

A county with a massive unorganized workforce scores high regardless of sector composition. This is intentional: organizing potential is a prerequisite for any strategy, even if the sectoral terrain is only moderate.

Known Data Gap
⚠️ Union membership data from DOL LM-2 filings has approximately 2,192 unresolvable union locals — records with PO boxes, international addresses, U.S. territories, or addresses that cannot be geocoded to a county FIPS. These represent roughly 20% of all LM-2 filers by count. This means the estimated union member count for any county is a floor, not a ceiling. The unresolvable records are a confirmed permanent exclusion — not a data error. The model treats this as a known limitation and documents it explicitly.

Scoring Component 6
Statewide Sub-score
Plain Language

The Statewide Sub-score measures how much organizing in a county can move the needle on statewide electoral contests. Counties in competitive states score higher; counties in safely blue or safely red states score lower, because the electoral marginal value of organizing there is lower.

Data sources: MIT Election Lab historical results; Cook Political Report competitiveness ratings for Senate and gubernatorial races.

Critical Caveat
⚠️ The Statewide Sub-score must be interpreted differently depending on the target race. Presidential, gubernatorial, and Senate races each trigger different intervention logic — they are NOT interchangeable even within the same state. A county that is critical for a Senate race may be irrelevant for a gubernatorial race, depending on the district geography and voter composition. Cycle-specific race targeting is a planned Phase 2 refinement. Users should apply organizer judgment to distinguish race types when using this sub-score.
Scoring Component 7
Congressional Sub-score
Plain Language

The Congressional Sub-score measures the electoral value of organizing in a county for House of Representatives races. Counties in genuinely competitive districts score highest. The score is derived from Cook Political Report district ratings and MIT Election Lab district-level historical results.

Design Decision: Primary Challenges

This model treats primary challenges as a legitimate strategic option. Safe Democratic districts with out-of-step incumbents are scored higher than equivalent safe Republican districts — because a primary challenge in a safe Democratic seat has a plausible path to changing representation, whereas organizing in a safe Republican seat has no near-term electoral payoff for a House flip strategy.

This is an explicit normative choice embedded in the framework. Users who prefer to exclude primary targeting can filter by Intervention Type to focus only on general-election-competitive districts.

Scoring Component 8
Infrastructure Score
Two Sub-Components

Organized Scale — Total union members in the county (raw count, scaled to 0–100). Measures the volume of existing organized power available for political deployment. High scale enables Type B and Type C interventions.

Union Culture — Union members as a percentage of total county workforce (density). Measures how normalized collective action is — whether the social infrastructure for organizing exists culturally. Relevant for Type A interventions: a county where workers have neighbors and family members in unions is culturally primed for new organizing.

Why Infrastructure is Kept Separate

The Infrastructure Score is deliberately excluded from the Organizing Opportunity Score formula. Union density is a lagging indicator — it is an outcome of past organizing strategy, not a predictor of future organizability. Including it would create a circular feedback: high-density counties would appear to be good organizing targets simply because they were organized in the past.

More importantly: high density can mask a shift toward transactional servicing unionism. A county where unions are large but politically passive and internally undemocratic may score high on infrastructure while being a poor candidate for new power-building. Infrastructure feeds Intervention Type classification; it does not elevate the organizing opportunity signal.

Do NOT use union density as a proxy for any boolean field in the Strategic Value Score rubric. These are distinct measurements.
Scoring Component 9
Intervention Type (A / B / C)
Classification Logic

Type A — Build New Power: Infrastructure score low, opportunity score high. The terrain is favorable for organizing but existing union presence is weak. Strategy: enter the sector or geography and build from scratch. Longest ROI, highest resource intensity.

Type B — Wake the Giant: Infrastructure score high, political alignment low. Unions exist but aren't activated. Strategy: mobilize existing members into durable electoral engagement. Medium ROI, medium resource intensity.

Type C — Move Together: Infrastructure score high, political alignment high. Conditions are aligned. Strategy: coordinate across locals and sectors to maximize voter contact and issue pressure. Shortest deployment timeline, lowest resource intensity per impact unit.

Resource Allocation Logic

Type A requires the most resources (staff, time, money) and produces the longest ROI — 3–7 years to meaningful electoral impact. Type C requires the least marginal investment and can produce electoral results within a single cycle. Type B sits between: existing infrastructure lowers the cost but political alignment work takes sustained effort. A complete portfolio strategy deploys across all three types, not just the shortest-return quadrant.

Theoretical Foundation
McAlevey Framework
Theoretical Grounding

This model is grounded in Jane McAlevey's whole-worker organizing framework. McAlevey argues that only sectors with genuine chokepoint leverage — the ability to create a crisis through collective action — combined with community embeddedness — deep roots in the social fabric beyond the workplace — can build durable political power. Her sector selection argument (organize healthcare and education workers, not gig workers or logistics temps) is operationalized directly in the Strategic Value Score rubric.

The Boolean fields for Chokepoint Potential, Community-Facing, and McAlevey Priority directly encode her framework. The ×1.15 multiplier for sectors scoring YES on both Chokepoint and Community-Facing reflects her core theoretical claim: the combination is more powerful than either dimension alone.

Key Citations

McAlevey, J. (2016). No Shortcuts: Organizing for Power in the New Gilded Age. Oxford University Press.

McAlevey, J. (2020). A Collective Bargain: Unions, Organizing, and the Fight for Democracy. Ecco.

Silver, B. (2003). Forces of Labor: Workers' Movements and Globalization Since 1870. Cambridge University Press. [Chokepoint theory alignment — Silver's associational/marketplace power distinction maps closely onto McAlevey's leverage framework.]

SSI vs. McAlevey Tension

The Strategic Sector Initiative (SSI) — an alternative labor strategy framework — prioritizes manufacturing as the organizing target, arguing that industrial workers have the greatest economic leverage and organizing tradition. McAlevey, by contrast, prioritizes social reproduction sectors (healthcare, education, public sector) as having both structural leverage and the community embeddedness necessary for durable political power.

This model sides with McAlevey. The SVS rubric reflects her sector hierarchy. Users who prefer an SSI framework would need to recode the McAlevey Priority field and upweight the Manufacturing – Durable sector scores. The Decisions Log documents this choice explicitly as a contested theoretical commitment, not a neutral methodological judgment.

Theoretical Context
Two-Actor Negotiating Logic
The Core Distinction

Labor strategy operates across two fundamentally different actor types, each responding to a different logic of pressure:

Capitalists are profit-maximizing actors with a legible cost-benefit calculus. A strike that disrupts production creates real, measurable losses. Conventional labor leverage — the credible threat of economic disruption — applies cleanly here. The Sectoral Value Score's Chokepoint and Crisis-Creating fields operationalize this logic.

Political actors respond to electoral, ideological, and reputational incentives. They can be ideologically immovable in ways that capitalists typically cannot afford to be. Scott Walker and Ron DeSantis are exemplary cases: both accepted enormous economic costs (public sector dysfunction, business community opposition) to pursue ideological anti-labor agendas because their reelection calculus did not depend on material outcomes for workers.

The electoral sub-scores reflect this distinction. When the goal is political transformation rather than collective bargaining wins, the pressure point shifts from economic disruption to electoral math. The model's two-layer architecture — organizing terrain separate from electoral scoring — encodes this distinction structurally.

Reference

Walker, S. (2014). The Untold Story of How the Koch Brothers Are Reshaping American Politics. [Documentary evidence on ideologically immovable political actors where conventional labor leverage failed to produce expected concessions.]

Epistemological Boundary
What This Model Cannot Do
Structural Blind Spots

This model operates on structural, publicly available data. It cannot see inside organizations, communities, or workplaces. The following are explicitly outside its scope — they require organizer judgment and on-the-ground intelligence:

Not measured: organic leader networks · associational power · employer-specific vulnerability · local political dynamics · reform caucus presence · recent organizing activity · internal union democracy · worker sentiment

Documented Limitations (from Decisions Log)

1. Progressive infrastructure / associational power absent. The model has no signal for DSA chapters, labor councils, interfaith coalitions, or other associational infrastructure. On-the-ground intel fills this gap.

2. Public sector employment understated. CBP excludes government employment. Government-heavy counties (DC, state capitals) have understated sectoral scores. BLS QCEW provides partial correction.

3. 28 counties missing election data. Primarily Alaska borough units. Documented, not errors.

4. ~2,192 unresolvable union locals. PO boxes, international addresses, territories — approximately 20% of LM-2 filers by count. Confirmed permanent exclusion ceiling. Union member counts in every county are floors, not ceilings.

5. Strike history not yet scored. Cornell ILR Labor Action Tracker data (2019–2024) is acquired but not incorporated. Planned Phase 2 addition.

6. Organizability rubric is draft. Flagged for deeper theoretical development. The Harris critique — that some organic leaders identified by standard whole-worker methods may be pro-management — is a live validity threat to the McAlevey framework as applied here.

7. Statewide sub-score is not race-specific. Gubernatorial vs. Senate vs. presidential races require different intervention logic. Cycle-specific data is a Phase 2 refinement.

8. EMS not yet a separate sector. Emergency Medical Services are currently folded into Healthcare – Ambulatory (SVS=10). EMS workers have genuine chokepoint leverage and should score SVS=100. Phase 2 addition.

Section 4

Limitations & What This Model Cannot Do

Every model has a boundary. This one's boundaries are documented here in full. These are not disclaimers — they are specifications of what organizer judgment must supply.

1
Progressive infrastructure and associational power — absent from model
The model has no signal for DSA chapters, labor councils, interfaith coalitions, tenant organizations, or other associational infrastructure. These networks are often the difference between a county that looks good on paper and one that is actually ready to organize. On-the-ground intel fills this gap.
2
Public sector employment understated
The County Business Patterns dataset excludes government employees by design. Government-heavy counties — DC, state capitals, county seats — will show lower Sectoral Value Scores than their actual structural importance warrants. BLS QCEW provides partial correction but does not fully resolve the gap.
3
28 counties missing election data
Primarily Alaska borough-level units that do not correspond to standard county-level election reporting. These are documented gaps, not data errors. Affected counties are flagged in the dataset with null values for all electoral sub-scores.
4
~2,192 unresolvable union locals (~20% of LM-2 filers)
Records with PO boxes, international addresses, U.S. territories, or addresses that cannot be geocoded to a county FIPS. This is a confirmed permanent exclusion ceiling — union member counts in every county are floors, not ceilings. The model does not attempt to impute or distribute these records.
5
Strike history not yet scored
Cornell ILR Labor Action Tracker data (2019–2024) is acquired but not yet incorporated into the model. A county's recent strike history is a meaningful signal of organizing culture and employer vulnerability that this model currently misses.
Phase 2 addition
6
Organizability rubric is draft — live validity threat
The organizability sub-rubric (distinct from the full SVS) is flagged for deeper theoretical development. The Harris critique — that some workers identified as organic leaders by standard whole-worker organizing methods may be pro-management — is a live validity threat to the McAlevey framework as operationalized here. This threat is not resolved in Version 1.0.
7
Statewide sub-score is not race-specific
Gubernatorial, Senate, and presidential races each trigger different intervention logic, different voter geographies, and different pressure strategies. The current Statewide Sub-score does not distinguish between them. Cycle-specific race targeting is a planned refinement.
Phase 2 refinement
8
EMS not yet a separate sector
Emergency Medical Services workers are currently folded into Healthcare – Ambulatory (SVS=10). EMS workers have genuine chokepoint leverage — ambulance work stoppages create immediate public crises — and should score SVS=100 by the model's own rubric. This misclassification understates the strategic value of counties with large EMS workforces.
Phase 2 addition
